without becoming sensational.

SECTION 1 — INVESTIGATION & ARREST
----------------------------------

1. TRACY EDWARDS AND THE ESCAPE
-------------------------------
Refined content:

On the night of July 22, 1991, 32-year-old Tracy Edwards escaped from
Dahmer's apartment with a handcuff attached to one wrist. At about 11:30 p.m.,
Milwaukee police officers Rolf Mueller and Robert Rauth encountered Edwards,
who said he had been threatened with a knife. The officers accompanied him
back to the apartment to retrieve the key to the handcuff. The FBI confirms
that the encounter directly led police to Apartment 213 and Dahmer's arrest.

Edwards later testified that Dahmer had offered him money and beer to pose for
photographs, then became threatening after they entered the apartment. Edwards
described being held for several hours and said Dahmer threatened to eat his
heart. These details belong to the Edwards subsection as testimony, not as
objective narration.

Source priority:
- FBI: arrest sequence
- 1992 trial testimony / Washington Post / Los Angeles Times: Edwards account

Refinement:
- Remove theatrical wording such as "operational opening."
- Do not use "a man fled down a street with a handcuff" as the entire factual
  source; identify Edwards and the responding officers.
- "At approximately 11:30 p.m." is supported by the FBI.

2. POLICE RETURN TO APARTMENT 213
---------------------------------
Refined content:

When officers returned to Apartment 213 with Edwards, Dahmer met them at the
door. Edwards identified the apartment and told officers that Dahmer had
threatened him with a knife. One officer entered the bedroom to retrieve the
handcuff key. There he saw an open drawer containing Polaroid photographs and
recognized images of human bodies. The officers then arrested Dahmer and
called for additional investigative assistance.

The FBI records that the apartment contained numerous human remains and other
evidence connected to multiple victims.

Important refinement:
- Do not present every detail of the photographs in the main narrative.
- The "human bodies in various stages of dismemberment" description can be
  attributed to officer testimony / contemporaneous reporting.
- Avoid calling the apartment a "house of horrors" or "museum" in the article's
  own voice.

3. FORENSIC SEARCH AND EVIDENCE
-------------------------------
Refined content:

A comprehensive search of Apartment 213 recovered remains associated with
eleven victims, along with photographs, tools, and other evidence. The FBI
later performed DNA, chemical, biological, tool-mark, photographic and
computer examinations on evidence submitted by Milwaukee investigators.

For historical reference, contemporaneous reporting described the apartment as
containing seven skulls and four additional heads, preserved human remains,
and a large drum containing remains in an acid solution. These details should
be presented selectively and clinically.

The final article should NOT reproduce a long inventory of body parts.

Evidence presentation rule:
- Main article: summarize the categories of evidence.
- Optional expandable "Forensic evidence" module: provide a more detailed
  inventory, with citations.
- No graphic photographs.

Important source discrepancy:
Contemporary sources differ on the exact capacity stated for the large drum
(55 versus 57 gallons). Use "large drum" in normal prose; use the exact
capacity only when tied to a named source.

4. JEFFREY JENTZEN
------------------
Dr. Jeffrey Jentzen was Milwaukee County's chief medical examiner at the time.
In a later professional memoir/reflection, he described the case as feeling
like "dismantling a museum" rather than handling an ordinary death
investigation.

Final wording:
    "Jentzen later recalled the case as resembling the dismantling of a museum."

Important:
- This is a later recollection by Jentzen, not a contemporaneous quote that
  should be presented without attribution.
- Keep it optional and restrained because the website's tone does not depend
  on a dramatic quotation.

5. ARREST AND INITIAL INVESTIGATION
-----------------------------------
Refined content:

Dahmer was arrested at Apartment 213 on July 22, 1991. The discovery converted
what began as an assault/handcuff investigation into a major homicide
investigation.

The FBI soon offered forensic and identification assistance. Investigators
traced Dahmer's history across multiple states and locations, while laboratory
personnel examined evidence from the apartment.

Source:
FBI "Serial Killers, Part 7: Jeffrey Dahmer."

6. CONFESSIONS AND INTERVIEWS
-----------------------------
Refined content:

Beginning shortly after the arrest, Dahmer gave extensive statements to
Milwaukee homicide investigators. Detectives Patrick Kennedy and Dennis
Murphy interviewed him over roughly 60 hours. The interviews supplied a major
source for reconstructing the series of seventeen killings and identifying
victims whose remains or records had not initially been connected.

Contemporaneous trial reporting described more than 60 hours of interviews,
while Court TV's archive identifies Kennedy and Murphy as the detectives who
took the confession.

Important refinement:
- Avoid saying "fully cooperative" without attribution or qualification.
- Better:
    "Dahmer gave extensive and detailed statements to investigators."
- Avoid saying the confession alone "enabled forensic specialists to
  reconstruct" everything; it worked together with physical and documentary
  evidence.
- The FBI explicitly says investigators used both forensic work and Dahmer's
  confession to identify earlier victims.

7. IDENTIFICATION OF VICTIMS
----------------------------
The identification process should be described as a combined investigation:
- Dahmer's statements
- physical remains
- photographs
- missing-person records
- family reports
- forensic testing
- records from other jurisdictions

The FBI says its investigators traced Dahmer's trail across the United States
and internationally and checked whether he could be connected to unsolved
murders in Ohio, Florida and Germany.

SECTION 2 — TRIAL & LEGAL PROCEEDINGS
-------------------------------------

1. JANUARY 13, 1992 — PLEA
---------------------------
Refined content:

On January 13, 1992, Dahmer entered a plea of guilty but insane to the
fifteen Milwaukee murder charges.

Those fifteen charges were not all styled identically under Wisconsin law:
two were first-degree murder charges and thirteen were first-degree
intentional homicide charges because the killings occurred under different
versions of the law.

Important:
- Do not write "fifteen counts of first-degree intentional homicide."
- Do not say the trial began on January 13.
- January 13 was the plea hearing.
- Jury selection began January 27; opening statements and the sanity trial
  followed at the end of January.

2. WHAT THE PLEA MEANT
----------------------
Wisconsin's procedure meant the guilty plea removed the ordinary factual-guilt
phase. The remaining contested issue was whether Dahmer was legally
responsible for the killings because of mental disease or defect.

The relevant statutory formulation asks whether, because of mental disease or
defect, the defendant lacked substantial capacity either to appreciate the
wrongfulness of the conduct or to conform conduct to the requirements of law.

Important refinement:
- Do not label the standard simply as the "American Law Institute Model Penal
  Code standard" in the main article.
- Wisconsin law used language derived from the American Law Institute
  formulation, but the immediate legal authority was Wisconsin statute.
- For the webapp, call it the "Wisconsin mental-responsibility standard" and
  cite the statute/case materials.

3. SANITY TRIAL
---------------
Refined content:

Jury selection took place in late January, and the sanity trial proceeded with
testimony from detectives, psychiatrists, psychologists, forensic and other
witnesses. The prosecution argued that Dahmer understood his actions were
wrong and had exercised planning and concealment, while the defense argued
that severe mental disturbance and paraphilic compulsions impaired his ability
to control his behavior.

Important:
- Attribute arguments to the prosecution and defense.
- Do not present "he was rational" or "he was irrational" as the site's own
  psychological conclusion.
- Detailed psychiatric analysis belongs under:
    /topics/psychological-profile

4. TELEVISED TRIAL
------------------
The trial received extensive television coverage. Court TV carried most of
the proceedings live, while Milwaukee television also provided coverage.
Contemporary reporting described national interest and extensive press
attendance.

Do not say "broadcast internationally" unless referring specifically to
international news coverage rather than the courtroom broadcast itself.

5. RITA ISBELL / VICTIM IMPACT
------------------------------
Rita Isbell, sister of Errol Lindsey, delivered a victim-impact statement at
the February 17 sentencing hearing. Her confrontation with Dahmer became one
of the most remembered moments associated with the proceedings.

Important:
- This was a sentencing/victim-impact event, not simply "trial testimony."
- Keep it in Trial & Legal Proceedings because it is part of the sentencing
  record.
- The later Netflix recreation belongs in Media & Cultural Portrayal.

6. FEBRUARY 15, 1992 — SANITY VERDICT
-------------------------------------
The jury found Dahmer legally sane on each of the fifteen murder counts.
Contemporary reports state the vote was 10-2 on each count.

The verdict did NOT mean the jury had just determined that Dahmer committed
the killings; he had already pleaded guilty. The jury rejected the defense's
claim of legal insanity/mental disease or defect sufficient to excuse criminal
responsibility.

This distinction must be explicit on the page.

7. FEBRUARY 17, 1992 — SENTENCING
---------------------------------
Judge Laurence C. Gram Jr. sentenced Dahmer to fifteen consecutive life terms
in Wisconsin.

Wisconsin had no death penalty, so the consecutive life sentences were the
maximum punishment available under the circumstances.

8. MAY 1, 1992 — OHIO CASE
---------------------------
Dahmer was returned to Ohio and pleaded guilty to the 1978 murder of Steven
Hicks. An Ohio judge imposed an additional life sentence.

The combined result was sixteen murder convictions:
- fifteen in Wisconsin
- one in Ohio

Important:
- Do not use the unstable "940 years" or "957 years" arithmetic as the
  headline legal outcome.
- The stable facts are the fifteen consecutive Wisconsin life terms plus the
  additional Ohio life sentence.

SENSITIVITY / EDITORIAL RULES
----------------------------
- No graphic crime-scene photographs.
- No graphic descriptions in the main narrative.
- Forensic evidence should be summarized rather than catalogued sensationally.
- If exact evidence inventories are included, place them behind an optional
  expandable module with neutral labels.
- Use direct quotations sparingly.
- Attribute witness testimony and legal arguments.
- Do not turn prosecutor or defense characterizations into the site's own
  psychological conclusions.
- Avoid loaded phrases such as "house of horrors," "grotesque museum," "monster,"
  or "nightmare" in the site's own narrative voice.
- Historical quotes may be retained when they add documentary value and are
  clearly attributed.
