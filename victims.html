Biographical Reconstruction • The 17 Victims

The Victims: Biographical Reconstruction & Case Register

Between 1978 and 1991, seventeen men and boys were killed in Ohio and Wisconsin. For decades, true-crime media has reduced their lives to forensic evidence tallies and sensationalized headlines. This archive restores historical focus to who they were: their personal identities, talents, family bonds, and aspirations. Each individual is documented chronologically by their best-supported last-known contact, with transparent evidentiary reconciliation of official records.

Table 1 Notation & Conventions
  • * Asterisk: Indicates a documented birth-date or age-reference discrepancy. Specifically, in 1991 news wire reporting, journalists frequently reported victims' ages at the time of Dahmer's post-arrest discovery rather than calculating age at disappearance (e.g., Richard Guerrero, Raymond Smith, Edward Smith, Ernest Miller, David Thomas, Curtis Straughter), or primary investigative files contained internal date variations (Steven Hicks, Steven Tuomi, James Doxtator, Jeremiah Weinberger, Oliver Lacy). Unmarked ages reflect clean agreement between documented primary DOB and displayed age.
  • Provisional Dagger Notation: Permanently retired across the archive following primary verification of David Thomas's official birth date (Dec. 21, 1967; age 22 years, 9 months at disappearance) in FBI Vault records. All seventeen victims are anchored by verified primary birth records.
  • Chronological Ordering: Governed strictly by the best-supported last-known contact date, not judicial count order or charging schedules.
  • 4-Milestone Separation: The archive rigorously distinguishes Last Known Sighting (Milestone 1), Missing-Person Report Date (Milestone 2), Alleged Offense Date (Milestone 3), and Forensic Identification Date (Milestone 4).

Victim 01 of 17 • 1978 Series Commencement Primary Skeletal & Dental ID

Steven Mark Hicks

Age: 18* • DOB: June 22, 1959 (Pt 03 p. 51; Pt 01 p. 39) / June 27, 1959 (Pt 01 p. 25) • Demographics: White • Last Known: June 18, 1978

Biographical Profile & Remembrance

Steven Mark Hicks was a recent high-school graduate from Coventry Township, Ohio. His father, Richard Hicks, described him as a deeply empathetic and gentle young man; in an Associated Press account, his father recalled that Steven had become inconsolably distraught after shooting a rabbit on a hunting trip, illustrating his aversion to violence. Steven was an avid music enthusiast and was hitchhiking toward a rock concert at Chippewa Lake Park when he accepted a ride from Dahmer. [1]

Evidentiary & Case Milestones (4-Milestone Separation Model)
  • Milestone 1 — Last-Known Sighting / Contact: June 18, 1978. Accepted ride while hitchhiking toward Chippewa Lake Park concert. [2]
  • Milestone 2 — Missing-Person Report Date: June 24, 1978. Filed with Summit County Sheriff's Department, Ohio. [2]
  • Milestone 3 — Alleged Offense Date & Charge: June 18, 1978. Aggravated Murder (Ohio R.C. 2903.01, Case CR-91-08-1686). [3][5]
  • Milestone 4 — Forensic Identification Date & Method: Sept. 13–14, 1991. Forensic odontology & radiographic bone matching by Summit County coroner. [4][17]
  • Evidentiary Disposition & Physical Remains: Convicted in Ohio (16th life term, May 1992). Pulverized bone fragments recovered Bath Township, August 1991.

Why His Case Matters: The May 27 Institutional Failure

The murder of Konerak Sinthasomphone remains the most widely scrutinized institutional failure of the Dahmer case. Konerak was the younger brother of Sombat Sinthasomphone, whom Dahmer had sexually assaulted in September 1988—a conviction that had placed Dahmer on state probation [2][3]. Despite repeated 911 calls from Black neighborhood residents Sandra Smith and Nicole Childress pleading for medical intervention for an injured child, responding police officers accepted Dahmer's false statement that Konerak was an adult domestic partner, threatened bystanders with arrest, and returned the 14-year-old boy to Dahmer's custody [7][20][22].

Detailed documentary scrutiny of the police radio tapes, officer disciplinary hearings, and civil rights litigation is maintained under Analysis: Systemic Failures and Impact: Social Reform.

Historiography & Archival Integrity

About the Record: Archival Limitations & Evidence Standards

The documentary reconstruction of the seventeen victims' lives is defined by the uneven nature of surviving municipal, federal, and journalistic archives. In keeping with rigorous historical scholarship, the archive operates under strict evidentiary boundaries.

1. Asymmetry of Surviving Records

Several victims' families—most notably those of Errol Lindsey, Anthony Hughes, and Konerak Sinthasomphone—spoke extensively with journalists and delivered profound courtroom statements, leaving rich personal and biographical memories. For others, surviving documentary trace is largely confined to missing-person dockets, latent fingerprint logs, and cold criminal complaints. The archive preserves these asymmetries transparently rather than manufacturing speculative narratives.

2. Source Hierarchy & Charging Evolution

Primary judicial records—including the initial July 25, 1991 four-count complaint [3][25], the expanding twelve-count filings [26][27], and the final 15-count preliminary information—form the baseline. Contemporary 1991 wire filings frequently recorded the victim's age at post-arrest discovery rather than recalculating age at disappearance, a systematic reporting artifact untangled in our master chronology.

3. Discrepancy Rules D-02 Through D-05

Four locked discrepancy rules govern this register: D-02 bars asserting a precise death date for Steven Tuomi due to uncharged corpus delicti; D-03 preserves dual dates for James Doxtator (Jan 1988 vs Spring 1988); D-04 preserves dual dates for Curtis Straughter (Feb 18 vs March 7, 1991); and D-05 links all 11 calculated age discrepancies to the Table 1 legend.
